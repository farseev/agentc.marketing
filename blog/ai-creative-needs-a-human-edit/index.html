Why AI creative needs a human edit, and what to cut

3 Aug 2026 · 6 min read · AgentC editorial team · FAQ

AI creative needs a human edit because agents optimise for patterns that worked before, which pulls every brand towards the same safe middle. A human editor supplies taste: choosing the option that is true to the brand, cutting what is merely competent, and sharpening what is distinctive. When options are abundant, the scarce skill is not making. It is choosing.

What goes wrong when agents produce creative unedited?

The output is rarely bad. It is usually fine, and that is the problem. Creative agents learn from what has worked, so they converge on familiar structures: the question hook, the three benefits, the urgent call to action. Run that across an entire category and you get a sea of sameness, which is exactly the complaint marketers voice about AI-generated content. Everyone sounds the same, and much of it still needs heavy human revision.

There is a second problem. Agents do not know what a brand should refuse to say. They know what it has said. Taste includes the no.

What does a creative agent do well?

Credit where due. Creative agents at AgentC are excellent at:

  • Producing many angles on a single audience tension, quickly.
  • Adapting an approved idea across formats and placements.
  • Keeping the audience's own language from research, rather than drifting into marketing-speak.
  • Generating deliberately unusual options when asked, which a human can then judge.

How should a strategist edit agent creative?

We use a five-pass routine. It takes less time than it sounds, because each pass removes options.

The five-pass human edit

  1. Truth pass: cut anything the brand cannot stand behind, including implied claims.
  2. Tension pass: cut anything that does not clearly address the audience tension in the brief.
  3. Sameness pass: cut anything a competitor could run unchanged by swapping the logo.
  4. Humanity pass: cut anything that feels manipulative, intrusive or cold, even if it would perform.
  5. Sharpen pass: rewrite the survivors by hand. One word changed by a person often does more than ten more variants.

The sameness pass is the most important and the most skipped. If you do one thing differently after reading this, do that one.

Agents give you a hundred competent options. Your job is to find the one that could only be yours.

Is taste just personal preference?

No, though it is often dismissed that way. Taste in marketing is judgement about fit: between message and brand, between tone and moment, between what will work and what will last. It is built from experience, exposure and knowing the audience. It can be argued for with reasons, which is how we distinguish it from whim.

That also means taste can be taught. Junior strategists at AgentC sit in on edit sessions and defend their cuts. Over time they learn to see sameness faster than any model can describe it.

Should creative be scored before it is edited?

Scoring helps, but it should inform the edit, not replace it. Predictive scoring of creative clickability before media spend is useful for ranking survivors of the edit and for avoiding expensive dead ends. Our sister app FMedia uses SOMIN's pre-spend clickability prediction for exactly this. But a score will not tell you whether a line is true to the brand or merely effective. That stays human.

What does a worked example look like?

A fitness brand's research loop surfaces a tension: people want to train consistently but feel judged in gyms. The creative agent drafts forty lines. The truth pass removes six that imply results. The tension pass removes twelve that drift into generic motivation. The sameness pass removes fifteen that any gym could run. The humanity pass removes two that lean on body insecurity, which would likely perform and which the brand should never run. Five survive. The strategist rewrites two by hand, and those two go to test.

Where does this leave creative teams?

In a better position than they fear, if they lean into editing. The craft shifts from producing to curating and sharpening, which is closer to what senior creatives already do. Teams that resist editing and simply ship agent output will struggle to tell their work apart from everyone else's.

How many options should agents produce?

Fewer than you think. It is tempting to ask for two hundred variants because it costs almost nothing, but a reviewer cannot genuinely judge two hundred lines, and the edit becomes skimming. We usually ask for twenty to forty options per tension, with a deliberate spread: some close to the brief, some stretching it, a few intentionally strange. That spread gives the editor real choices rather than two hundred shades of the same idea.

If the editor finds nothing worth keeping, the answer is rarely more volume. It is a better brief, usually with clearer examples of what good looks like.

One more habit worth adopting: keep a short file of rejected lines and the reason for each rejection. Over a few months it becomes the most honest description of your brand's taste you will ever have, far more useful than adjectives in a brand book. It also makes a superb set of negative examples for the next agent brief.

Frequently asked questions

Why does AI-generated creative all look the same?

Creative agents learn from patterns that have worked before, so they converge on familiar structures. Without human editing for distinctiveness, brands drift towards a shared middle.

What is the sameness test?

A check in AgentC's editing routine: if a competitor could run the line unchanged by swapping the logo, cut it or rewrite it.

Can creative scoring replace human editing?

No. Scoring helps rank options by likely performance, but it cannot judge brand truth, distinctiveness or whether a message is humane. Those stay with a human editor.
